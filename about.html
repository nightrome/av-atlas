What AV Atlas is

AV Atlas indexes autonomous vehicle (AV) research papers from major computer vision and robotics venues and reports who is publishing, which institutions and countries are active, how research topics have shifted over time, and which papers are most cited within AV research specifically. Unless a control says otherwise ("All papers" vs. "AV papers"), every statistic on the site is computed over the filtered corpus of AV papers only. Citation numbers are therefore roughly 13× lower than Google Scholar's; we think they are a better gauge of standing within the AV field.

Every classification here, whether a paper is AV-relevant, which topic it belongs to, who wrote it, or who cites it, is produced by an automated pipeline, not manual review. Figures should be read as a well-informed estimate, not a verified count, and no confidence intervals are shown: where a leaderboard row or rollup rests on only a handful of papers, the paper count shown next to it is the guide to how much weight it can bear. Venue and year coverage is uneven (see Data coverage), so counts over time and comparisons between venues or between authors are shaped partly by how completely each venue-year is indexed — read trends as directional, not exact. The methodology's shortcomings are described below. To correct or remove your information, get in touch.

How it works

Every figure on this site is built by an automated pipeline from public paper listings; no paper is curated in by hand. The pipeline runs in five stages, plus one feedback loop: when stage 4 finds a paper that cites one already in the corpus, that paper is added and run through all five stages itself.

Paper listings come from each venue's own official proceedings pages wherever possible. When a venue's own pages can't be read automatically, or are missing for some years, a public backup listing of the same proceedings is used instead — for a handful of venues and journals, that backup only has title, authors and year, with no abstract (see Data coverage below for exactly which). A separate public lookup, not a venue listing itself, provides the citation links between papers and the stable author IDs used later on.

  • 1. Collect. Pull the complete proceedings of every covered venue and year from the sources above. No keyword filter: every paper from a covered venue is included, AV-related or not, so the site can measure how large a slice of each venue is AV work.
  • 2. Merge & classify. Dedupe across sources by normalized title. Assign a category (topic, e.g. object detection, planning) from keyword matching, and an AV-relevance label independently, against a written rubric: a paper is an AV paper if its own contribution or evaluation is about automating a road vehicle or the driving task, and a non-AV paper if it only names autonomous driving as one possible application. A layered classifier decides it: hard scope filters first exclude non-road platforms (aerial, underwater, legged, manipulation) and pure vehicle-hardware papers; then an explicit AV-specific phrase in the title or abstract, or a standalone driving word in the title, marks a paper as AV; then a small model of learned per-phrase weights (trained on hand labels plus labels from a locally-run language model, with negative weights on phrases that over-fire) and a verdict from that same local language model get the last say on the rest. A paper's category never feeds this decision, since category keywords are generic CV/robotics terms that match plenty of non-AV papers.
  • 3. Enrich. For AV-relevant papers only: each author's affiliation and a stable ID that tells apart authors who share a name are looked up where that information can be found, plus profile photos for top-ranked authors.
  • 4. Citation graph. For every paper whose full text is available, its reference list is checked against the rest of the corpus to work out which papers cite which. Separately, we check whether any paper that isn't in the corpus yet cites one that already is. If a full copy of that new paper can also be found, it's added and goes through the same process as every other paper (the feedback loop above). If no full copy can be found, neither the paper nor its citation is added — there'd be no way to say anything reliable about a paper we can't actually read.
  • 5. Aggregate. Leaderboards, per-author/institution/country rollups, and category breakdowns are computed and published to every page on this site.

A note on preprints. Conferences and journals each publish a fixed, known set of accepted papers every year — that fixed list is what stage 1 collects, in full. Papers that only ever appeared as preprints, with no peer review and no such fixed list, are not collected that way. A preprint is only included if it was also published at a covered venue (as a source of full text), or if it's found through the citation graph in stage 4 because something already in the corpus cites it.

Two independent checks against real ground truth, not one automated method checked against another.

63 of the 65 were classified the way the reviewer labeled them. Zero false positives: every paper the site calls AV-relevant, a human agreed was AV-relevant. The false negatives were borderline computer-vision papers that mention autonomous driving as one of several possible applications rather than being about it.

Citation counts vs. Google Scholar

i

Every citation count here is internal: how many other papers already in this corpus cite a given paper, computed entirely from data collected for this site. It measures something specific (impact within AV research, not research in general), not a global count. External citation databases frequently disagree with each other and generally cannot be redistributed, so this site doesn't use them as a source.

Every point sits above the dashed y=x line: Scholar's count is higher for every single paper in the sample, since Scholar counts citations from anywhere on the internet, while this site only counts citations from other papers already in its own corpus. The gap is fairly consistent on a log scale, which is why "roughly an order of magnitude higher on Scholar" is a reasonable mental model, as long as it is treated as rough. A "no citation data" paper isn't necessarily uncited. It may be a paper no other paper in this corpus cites yet, or one whose own reference list hasn't been processed yet (see What's not covered yet).

This site's data-collection processes run on an ongoing basis; coverage only increases over time. The table is the current status of each stage as of the last data update.

  • Some venues have no abstracts at all (see the Abstracts column under Data coverage). AV-relevance and category for those rest on title text alone, a weaker signal than the rest of the corpus.
  • A meaningful share of papers are labeled "Uncategorized." The topic taxonomy does not yet cover every subject area; not all of these are miscategorized, some simply fall outside the current category list.
  • No author disambiguation. Name-only matching merges distinct people who share a name and splits one person across name-spelling variants. A name whose papers carry more than one distinct external author identifier is flagged and left out of the leaderboards automatically; that only catches confirmed conflicts, not every merged name.
Download the data

The whole corpus, as four gzipped CSVs. Every number on this site is computed from these, so anything here can be checked, extended, or plotted a different way without re-running the pipeline. paper_id joins the tables.

  • av-atlas-papers.csv.gz — one row per AV paper: title, year, venue, category, in-corpus citations, institutions, countries.
  • av-atlas-authorship.csv.gz — paper-to-author rows, with author position.
  • av-atlas-citations.csv.gz — the in-corpus citation edge list.
  • av-atlas-institutions.csv.gz — institutions with country, sector and totals.
  • README.md — column definitions and, more importantly, what these numbers are not.

Read the README before using them. Citation counts are internal to this corpus and incomplete; affiliations are resolved for a minority of papers; author names are not disambiguated. Privacy

This site indexes bibliographic metadata (titles, author names, affiliations, venues, years, and citation relationships) about published academic papers. It does not collect any information about visitors to the site. If you are an author and believe your information is displayed inaccurately, or you'd like your name, affiliation, or photo removed or corrected, use the "Report an error on this page" button (🚩) in the navigation bar, or click here. No account or sign-up required, and this applies equally to a data correction and a privacy takedown request.

Source code and design notes

Everything here is built by an open repository: the crawlers, the classifier, the aggregation, and these pages. Two files in it are worth reading if you want to judge the method rather than take it on trust — DECISIONS.md, which records why each non-obvious choice was made and what it was measured against, and RUBRIC_relevance.md, the written definition of AV-relevant that the classifier and the hand-labelled evaluation both work from.
